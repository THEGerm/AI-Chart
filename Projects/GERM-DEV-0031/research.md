# Senet: what the evidence says and whether it plays

Senet cannot be played from the ancient evidence alone. The sources fix the board, the path, the pieces, the lot and the marked squares. No ancient source gives the throw values, the start, how pieces interact or how they leave the board. Add 4 to 6 invented rules (throw values, start, capture mechanism, bearing off) and it becomes a working game; every published reconstruction is that. Three of the four published rule sets that use sticks take their core mechanics from tab, a stick game first named in Cairo in 1310 CE and first scored by Thomas Hyde in 1694, some 2,000 years after the last securely dated senet board. No documented line runs from senet's sticks to tab. In 400 seeded computer games per rule set, all five rule sets in the app finish every game. Kendall, Jéquier, Piccione and a minimal set are balanced within about 5 points. Bell's rules give one seat 75% of the wins. The strategy depth comes from the invented interaction rules.

## 1. Studies

Crist, Dunn-Vaturi and de Voogt (2016) is the current synthesis, and it treats every modern rule set as a reconstruction. No peer-reviewed paper after 2016 proposes a full rule set or argues for or against capture. The recent work is on chronology and diffusion.

## 2. What is known

Crist (2019: 107) calls senet "the most studied and best understood of ancient board games, and yet much remains unknown about it". The name is zn.t (TLA lemma 136150); znt n(t) ḥꜥb, "game of passing", comes from Faulkner's *Concise Dictionary* and Coptic sine "passing" (secondary).

**Board and path (evidenced).** The board is 3 x 10, played boustrophedon from top left to bottom right, from Hesy-Re to the Late Period (Crist 2019: 108). Numbering the squares 1 to 30 is inferred from the Great Game Text and pTurin 1775. Seven Middle Kingdom and Second Intermediate boards (Dyn 12-17) are turned 180 degrees, with the marked squares top left (Crist 2019: 108, 112).

**Marked squares (evidenced).** Square 26 is nfr / nfrw, "good". Square 27 carries an X or water (n, mw, Hapy, hippos). Square 28 is always "three" (strokes, men, gods, ba birds). Square 29 is always "two". The canonical sequence is good, water, three, two (Crist 2019: 108-110). Examples: BM 21576 (Hatshepsut tomb), Leiden AH 34a (Baky, Thutmose III), Rosicrucian RC 1261.

Square 15 carries an X on Middle Kingdom boards (Dyn 12 Abydos board). On New Kingdom boards it is the square outside 26-30 most often decorated: checkers, a rosette, or the ankh-was group on Tutankhamun's board (Tait 1982 fig 1; Crist et al. 2016: 54). The Turin game text calls it the "house of repeating life". Piccione (1980: 56) gives it that name, with a frog and an extra turn. "House of Rebirth" is a modern gloss.

Square 30 is rarely marked before Dyn 19: one Dyn 18 board shows birds in papyrus, Dyn 19 a sun disk, Dyn 20 Horus (Piccione 1990: 242-247, via Crist et al. 2016: 54). Old Kingdom boards carry "one" on 30 (Hesy-Re; Seshemnefer IV graffito). Piccione (1980) assigns 30 to Re-Horakhty and has the game finish when every piece crosses it.

A photograph of the box as displayed at the Grand Egyptian Museum shows Tutankhamun's ebony box on its sledge stand, Carter 345 / JE 62058. Square 15 carries the ankh-was group, 26 three nfr signs, 27 three water lines, 28 three birds, 29 two seated men, and 30 is plain. The layout fits the boustrophedon reading: 15 is 6th from the left in the middle row, and 26 to 30 are the last five squares of the bottom row.

**Pieces (evidenced).** Cones and spools appear across the whole history (Crist 2021: 18). Early Dynastic and Old Kingdom play used 7 a side (Hesy-Re, 2 x 7). By Dyn 18 it was 5 a side (Piccione 1980; Crist et al. 2016: 67). Tutankhamun's box 585r (JE 62061, solid ivory, 13.5 x 4.2 x 2.8 cm) has a drawer never opened in antiquity. Carter's card (Griffith Institute) lists "two ivory knuckle-bones; five ivory coloured red playing pieces ... and five white ivory playing pieces ... making undoubtedly a complete set". It is the only firm complete New Kingdom count: 5 + 5.

The other Tutankhamun boxes are incomplete. Box 393 (JE 62060) has 4 + 5 pieces, "one missing", and 1 knucklebone, "one missing". Box 345 (JE 62058) has 2 ivory knucklebones and 2 pairs of throwsticks (17.5 cm, finger ends and fox heads); the only pieces near it are 4 + 4 in faience, and Carter doubted they belong. Box 593 (JE 62059) has groups of 5 ivory pieces, 2 knucklebones and two pairs of sticks. Carter cards 345-07/08 are his copy of Lane's tab and seega rules, which are modern and carry no ancient evidence. The Met's Abydos board (01.4.1) came with 7 spools and 8 cones: excavated sets are often incomplete or have extras. Curators arrange the pieces on displayed boards, so a museum layout is no evidence of a start position.

**Lot (evidenced).** Four two-sided throwsticks appear at Hesy-Re and in Tutankhamun's tomb. Knucklebones (astragali) are found paired with sticks in New Kingdom boxes (Met 16.10.475; Tutankhamun boxes). No source says how either was scored. The caption "ḥꜥb 5", "playing five", occurs at Beni Hasan and in Old Kingdom scenes (Jéquier 1922: 18; Kendall 1982: 265; Crist et al. 2016: 51, 85). The word ḏbꜥ, "finger", recurs in captions and may mean throwsticks (Kendall 1982: 271).

**Players (evidenced).** Of 15 Old Kingdom depictions, 9 show two players (Crist et al. 2016: 45-47). In Book of the Dead 17 the dead plays an unseen opponent in a booth. Nefertari (QV66) plays alone against an unseen opponent; sources disagree whether the scene belongs to BD 17 or spell 148.

**Captions (text evidenced, reading inferred).** These are the words spoken by players in tomb scenes.

- Pepiankh, Meir, Dyn 6. A: "It has alighted. Be happy my heart, for I shall cause you to see it taken away." B: "You speak as one weak of tongue, for passing is mine." (Piccione 1980: 55; Blackman 1924.)
- Nebkauhor: "Take this one off. Hurry, fool." Reply: "I will indeed cause that your finger leads the way to the house of the three bones." The negation is unsettled; it may read "Do not take this one off" (Motte, citing Pusch 1979 pl 6).
- Idu, Giza, Dyn 6: "I cause my finger to be led to the house of the plough/ibis" (Simpson 1976: 25). Piccione reads "house of penetration/humiliation".
- Nebenmaat, New Kingdom: "sitting in the pavilion, playing senet, knowing three and finding two" (Piccione 1980).

**Game texts (text evidenced, reading inferred).** The Great Game Text survives in pTurin Cat. 1775 verso, pCairo JdE 58037 and the tomb of Inherkhau (TT 359), all Dyn 20 and within two generations (Piccione 1990: 97-106). The Turin text, translated by J. Jüngling for the Museo Egizio, reads in part: "[His] draughtsmen shall turn around"; "His fingers are confused"; "I shall pass ... to the house of repeating life, while my opponent is held back in the house of nets"; "I shall hold him back from the per-nefer; I shall take my draughtsmen so that I may find my house"; "my seven are with the wind, in front of my fingers". It ends "[You] are true of voice!" and names the houses of nets, repeating life, water, bread, pouring and the per-nefer. Another text quoted by Piccione (1980: 56) runs: "I seize his game pieces so that he might drown together with his game pieces. I throw him into the water." Piccione reads this as square 27, with the piece removed from the board. In the New Kingdom senet becomes religious, and the opponent is drowned when the deceased wins (Crist et al. 2016: 55). No text read for this project describes entering the board, throw values or the exact capture mechanics.

**Date range (evidenced).** The earliest find is Abu Rawash tomb M1, Dyn 1 (c 3100-2900 BCE): 25 ivory squares, 3 marked like later senet squares (Crist 2021: 14). A three-row board appears as the hieroglyph mn from Narmer. The predynastic el-Mahasna 3 x 6 clay table is rejected (Crist 2021: 14). The first intact boards are from Early Bronze II Arad, Canaan, 3080-2700 BCE (Crist 2021: 14). The first full depiction is in the tomb of Hesy-Re, Dyn 3: a board with 2 x 7 domed pieces and 4 sticks (Quibell 1913; Crist et al. 2016: 44).

The latest unequivocal boards are Dyn 26 (664-525 BCE), scratched on terracotta platters at Tell Defenneh: BM EA22323 and two more (Crist 2019: 108). "Played until Roman times" is a factoid (Crist 2021). Disputed late cases: Petosiris, c 300 BCE (a 3 x 11 board), and Dendera roof graffiti (Roman, unpublished photographs). Arnette (BIFAO 121, 2021) publishes a Ptolemaic board fragment. P.Oxy. 470 (3rd century CE, Greek) describes a 30-square game with pieces called "dogs", squares called "houses", 26 the "good house" and 30 the "House of Horus"; Piccione identifies it as senet (Crist et al. 2016: 63-64; Finkel 2007). A Demotic text of the 2nd century CE in the Thesaurus Linguae Aegyptiae corpus speaks of "going down and up the fields" and "the thoughts of the board"; its edition was not identified. The texts, then, run later than the boards.

**Beyond Egypt (evidenced).** Boards occur in the Levant (Arad, Tell es-Safi, Byblos, Hama, Kamid el-Loz, Hazor), in periods of Egyptian presence. Cyprus has them from the end of the 3rd millennium to about 1000 BCE. Nubian finds are thin. Crete has three possible 3 x 10 stones, LM II (Crist 2021). Dunn-Vaturi, Crist and de Voogt (JAEI 2021) conclude that senet probably changed over about 3,000 years.

**Twenty squares.** The twenty-square game is common on the back of New Kingdom senet boxes, Tutankhamun's included (Crist 2019: 111). Finkel reads tablet BM 33333B (Babylon, 177-176 BC) as 5 bird-named pieces each, two astragali, rosettes safe and an exact throw to bear off. The tablet is over 2,000 years later than Ur and has no tie to Egyptian play.

Two gaps cut across the rest. Period drift means any single rule set is a composite: boards, piece counts and square marks span 2,500 years, and a rule built from a Dyn 6 caption and a Dyn 20 papyrus joins evidence from different games. Dependence on tab is the second. Three of the four published stick rule sets borrow tab's scoring, as Lane (1860) recorded it in Cairo. The specialists on tab place its origin in the Byzantine and early Islamic Near East: its 3 x 14 and 4-row boards first appear in the Levant and Arabia in the 5th to 8th centuries CE, and Depaulis (2024) holds they "cannot reasonably be held as senet". The game is first named in 1310 and first scored in 1694. The two sticks-and-race traditions share four two-sided lots and nothing else on record. The borrowing is a modern guess.

## 4. Second and third rounds of research

The second round settled six points.

- **Bell, checked at source.** *Board and Table Games* vol I, 2nd ed (1969) pp 27-28, "Suggested Rules for the Game of Thirty Squares", matches Ludii 529. Ten men are placed alternately on rows 1-2. One long die is used; the higher throw moves first. Landing on an enemy piece captures and removes it. No piece moves onto its own side's piece or moves backwards. Pieces on the squares marked X, III, II and I cannot be captured and leave on an exact throw. The exit-sign square is safe, but the piece must move on as soon as possible. Most pieces borne off wins.
- **Jéquier's citation.** Ludii cites G. Jéquier, "Un jeu renouvelé des anciens", *Chronique d'Égypte* 5 (1930) 124-127. Unverified at the journal.
- **Tab, read at source** (Lane 1860, ch XVII pp 346-349). Each stick has one white side and one "black". One white scores 1 ("tab"), 2 white 2, 3 white 3, 4 white 4, 4 black 6; there is no 5. Throws of 1, 4 and 6 throw again; 2 and 3 end the turn. The board (seega) has 4 rows of 7 to 15 squares, and the pieces are called "dogs". Every piece needs a tab (1) for its first move. Capture is by landing, pieces stack, and there is no bearing off. Jéquier's throws are tab exactly. Kendall's (0 = 5, extra throws on 1, 4, 5) are tab modified. Piccione's entry on a 1 is tab. Carter copied Lane's tab rules onto his own cards for box 345.
- **Ludii self-play metrics**, read on the portal (concepts.php and conceptsUCT.php) on 2026-11-22. The playout count is not stated, and the figures are not peer reviewed. Results are in section 5.
- **Method.** The Digital Ludeme Project uses self-play measures as plausibility indicators (Browne et al. 2019) and holds that higher-quality rule sets are more likely to have been played (Browne 2019). None of its method papers gives senet results. The Ludii Games Database splits the senet evidence by period into partial rule sets: Simple, Middle Kingdom, Early and Late New Kingdom, Vertical, Double, Cypriot (Crist, Stephenson, Piette and Browne 2024). Crist et al. (2026) apply the method to another board, Ludus Coriovalli (read through press coverage only).
- **Throw odds.** No physical throwing experiment on ancient-style sticks was found. The common odds (1: 25%, 2: 37.5%, 3: 25%, 4: 6.25%, 0: 6.25%) assume 50/50 sticks, an assumption no one has tested. Nogueira, Rodrigues and Trabucho model a stick as a half cylinder; their numbers were not reached. For astragali, Duggan, Bell and Taylor (2015) found David's odds (4/10 for each wide face, 1/10 for each narrow face) broadly correct on carpet (n = 2,000), with narrow faces rarer on wood (n = 3,000).

### Third round: Egyptian texts, Arabic scholarship and the later stick games

The third round searched three areas the first two missed: the Egyptian texts in the original language, scholarship in Arabic, and the record of stick games after senet.

**Egyptian texts.** The Thesaurus Linguae Aegyptiae corpus (read through its open data dumps; the website sits behind a bot check) gives the Old Kingdom vocabulary of play.

- ḥꜥb m zn.t, "playing at senet" (lemma 136150, Dyn 5).
- ḥꜥb ꞽr=k, "Go on, play!" (lemma 102160), and zbi̯ ꞽr=k, "Move, then!", with zbi̯ meaning "send out" a piece (lemma 131460). Masters (2024) places the first in the tomb of Kaiemankh at Giza.
- ꞽṯi̯, "take, seize": ꞽṯi̯.t m zn.t, "capturing (pieces) in senet", above two players in a tomb published by Quibell (Masters 2024, citing Miatello; the tomb is not named and the plate was not seen). Shore and Miatello read the verb as capturing a piece; Goede reads it as winning the game. On the first reading it is the strongest text yet that pieces could be taken.
- No word for throwstick, knucklebone or game piece occurs in the Earlier or Late Egyptian corpus.

The Demotic First Tale of Setne Khamwas (pCairo 30646, Ptolemaic) calls the game ḥbꜥ.t, "the game", and the pieces ꞽwꞽw, "dogs". Setne and the dead Naneferkaptah play three games for the Book of Thoth; each loss sinks Setne deeper into the ground. The tale says nothing about throws or moves. Piccione asked in a conference paper whether Setne played senet; that paper was not read. A Demotic text of the 2nd century CE, edition not identified, speaks of "going down and up the fields" of a board. In Coptic, ⲥⲓⲛⲉ means "pass by" (Crum 343b), which supports the "game of passing" etymology, and ⲃⲓϫ means "bones used as dice" (Crum 48b).

**Arabic scholarship.** No Arabic peer-reviewed study of senet itself was found. Arabic sources mention the game in surveys of ancient Egyptian pastimes and in press coverage of the Grand Egyptian Museum display, which says the exact rules "are still a mystery". The Egyptian Knowledge Bank journal servers refused connections from this project, so their articles were judged from search snippets and absence is unproven. The one Egyptian dissertation on senet is W.M. Refat, *Le jeu des trente cases dans l'Égypte ancienne* (Université libre de Bruxelles, 1972, supervised by A. Théodoridès), which Crist (2019) lists among the standard studies. Its contents are not online.

**The later stick games.** Tab is first named by the Cairo poet Ibn Dāniyāl (d. 1310) and first scored by Hyde (1694, pp 367-371 in the 1767 edition). Hyde's scoring differs from Lane's: 4 white scores 6 and 4 yellow scores 4, the reverse of Lane. Davies (Sudan Notes and Records 8, 1925) records Sudanese variants with three sticks. Tab-type boards appear from the 5th to 8th centuries CE at Horvat 'Aqav, Jerusalem, Hammat Gader and other Levantine and Arabian sites, and in early Islamic Morocco (Depaulis 2024; Penn, Fenwick and Limane 2026). The one Egyptian candidate, a 3 x 14 slab from Memphis (Petrie UC59257), is undated. No study derives tab from senet, and de Voogt, Hassanat and Alhasanat (2017) found no evidence for it in Roman or Nabataean sources.

**Effect on the gaps.** Capture (gap 6) gains a caption. Entry (gap 4) gains a weak hint. Throw values and extra throws (gaps 1 and 2) stay invented, and the usual model for them, tab, turns out to be a later, unstable and unrelated tradition. Period drift (gap 17) gains late texts that may or may not describe senet.

Still open after the third round: the Quibell plate with ꞽṯi̯.t m zn.t; the full Thesaurus attestation lists; the original wording of the Pepiankh, Nebkauhor, Idu and Beni Hasan captions and of pCairo JdE 58037 and Inherkhau; the edition of the Roman Demotic text; Piccione's paper on Setne; Refat 1972; the Egyptian Knowledge Bank journals; Murray 1952 and Rosenthal 1975 at source.

## 5. Is it playable?

Yes, with invented rules; no, from the evidence alone.

**Method.** The script `tests/playability.js` plays 400 seeded computer-against-computer games per rule set. Starts are alternated between seats. The computer players are heuristic (one ply, plus an expectimax level). Sticks are assumed 50/50 per face.

**Reading.** All five rule sets finish every game, with no stalls. Kendall, Jéquier, Piccione and Minimal are balanced within about 5 points.

Bell's is a short strike-out race of 45 throws with a strong seat bias: cones win 75%. Ten pieces start on rows 1-2, removal is permanent, the lot is a die, and the cones sit behind the spools and strike first. Ludii agrees: its UCT agent gives player 1 an advantage of 0.83, against our 75% for cones. Both projects find Kendall balanced.

Ludii's Jéquier leaves about a third of games unfinished; ours finishes all of them. Our Jéquier omits the end-of-game scoring, and Ludii's turn cap and draw handling are undocumented. Ludii counts turns and this project counts throws, so the game lengths do not compare one to one.

Minimal is playable but thin. 13% of throws have no move, only 55% offer a choice, and skill matters least (63%). The evidence alone, with the fewest inventions, gives a race game with little to decide. Capture plus protection (Kendall, Jéquier) gives the most decisions and the most room for skill.

**Verdict.** The known material is not playable on its own. It fixes the board, the path, the pieces, the lot and the marked squares. No ancient source gives throw values, how pieces interact or how they leave. With 4 to 6 invented rules (throw values, start, capture mechanism, bearing off) it becomes a working game, and every published reconstruction is that. Strategy depth comes from the invented interaction rules.

**Caveats.**

- The computer players are heuristic, so the skill figures are lower bounds.
- Sticks are assumed 50/50 per face. Real curved sticks may land otherwise; no throwing experiment has been published.
- Bell's rules in the simulation follow Ludii's encoding, which the 1969 text matches.
- The simulation found and fixed one bug: the Bell end-game tie-break first gave the win to the side wiped out.

### Rule sweep: which invented rules drive the results

**Method.** The folder `sweep/` holds a Python port of the engine, checked case by case against the JavaScript engine. It varies the 12 open rules of section 3 and plays every combination: 13,312 rule sets, 200 games of normal against normal and 200 of normal against a random mover each, with the same dice sequences across rule sets. The run took 17 sessions and 19 CPU hours, completed 2026-11-24, and passed every sanity check (parity, code lock, integrity, logic, determinism, anchors against the five published rule sets). The registry is `sweep/results/`.

**Measure.** For each result, the share of its variation across rule sets that one rule explains on its own (eta squared). A large share means the result comes from that modern choice. Certainty is from `rules.md`.

Skill is the win rate of the normal computer against a random mover, a lower bound. Balance is Ludii's measure (1 is fair). Decisions is the share of throws with 2 or more legal moves. Drama is a proxy: how far the eventual winner trailed in the race, on average, while behind.

**Findings.**

- **Unfinished games come from one invented rule.** 2,229 rule sets (16.7%) leave games unfinished within 3,000 throws. 2,208 of them use the bear-off rule that requires every piece in the last row before any leaves. With exits from 26 on exact throws, 100% of games finish; with the last-row rule, 85%. Rear capture and 7 pieces make it worse.
- **Skill comes from the capture mechanism.** Capture alone explains 48% of the variation in skill, and its pairing with the stop on 26 explains 59%. By mechanism, the normal player beats a random mover 75% of the time with no capture, 81% with a swap, 86% with removal and 98% when the struck piece goes to the rear. The mechanism has 20% certainty.
- **The evidenced rules matter little.** The number of pieces, the stop on 26 and square 27 explain 5% of skill or less each. Throw scoring, blockades and backward moves explain nothing measurable on any result.
- **Seat balance depends on capture and the start.** 10.8% of rule sets favour one seat by more than 10 points. Capture explains 22% of balance, and capture with the start 42%.
- **Pure luck is rare.** Only 1.1% of rule sets have skill under 60%. Almost any combination of rules leaves room for a better player.

**Reading.** The two results that decide whether a reconstruction works, whether games end and whether skill pays, rest on two of the least certain rules: how pieces leave and what a capture does. The rules with ancient support shape the game far less. The Germain Rules (`sweep/stage2-candidates.md`) replace exactly those two with the smallest rules the evidence suggests. A second stage will test them at 2,000 games each.

### Stage 2 test of the Germain Rules

**Rules.** The Germain Rules (Germain et al.) replace the two rules the sweep found decisive with the smallest rules the evidence suggests:

- **Capture:** a struck piece that had passed 15 goes back to 15, the house of repeating life, or the nearest free square below. Otherwise it goes off the board and enters again.
- **Leaving:** a piece leaves from 26 on a 5, from 28 on a 3, from 29 on a 2 and from 30 on a 1, by the exact throw only.

The rest: 5 pieces each, entering from off the board, a throw of 0 light faces scores 5, no extra throws, every piece stops on 26, and 27 sends a piece back to 15. There are three versions:

- **Fate:** the rearmost piece that can move must move.
- **Contest:** free choice, and two pieces side by side cannot be struck.
- **Master:** free choice of which piece to move; a lone piece can always be struck.

The names of Contest and Master were swapped on 2026-11-25, after the results below showed the unprotected version is the harder game.

Design and reasons: `sweep/stage2-candidates.md`.

**Method.** `sweep/stage2.py` plays every rule set in the app, read from the app's own rule file, at 2,000 games for each of three tests:

- **Balance:** normal against normal.
- **Skill:** normal against a random mover.
- **Look-ahead:** hard (one-ply expectimax) against normal.

Starts and seats alternate. The ± figures are 95% intervals. Results: `sweep/results/stage2.json`, run 2026-11-25 after the rename and with the Old Kingdom versions added.

**Against the pass marks set before the run:**

- **Every game ends.** Every Germain version finishes all 2,000 games. The longest game, Old Kingdom Master, takes 723 throws, well inside the 3,000 cap.
- **Balanced.** Cones win 48.8% to 51.7%, within the noise.
- **Fate is pure luck.** The normal player beats a random mover 51.2% of the time, which is chance. Fate has no decisions, so this confirms the rule works.
- **Skill rises with the unprotected version, against the prediction.** The design expected protection to add skill. The version without it has the most skill of the 5-piece rule sets: 99.1% against a random mover, and 66.8% for look-ahead against normal play. With pairs protected it drops to 95.2% and 54.6%. Protecting pairs cuts hits from 70 a game to 19, and with fewer targets there is less to plan. The names were swapped to match: Master is now the unprotected version.

**Old Kingdom versions.** The depictions (below and `depictions.md`) support 7 pieces a side and an alternating start in the Old Kingdom. With those two changes, Old Kingdom Master is the deepest game tested: look-ahead wins 71.3%, with 91% of throws offering a choice, at the cost of long games (390 throws). There is no Old Kingdom Fate: forced moves from an alternating start gave one seat 62% in a first test, and the solo scenes are New Kingdom.

**Reading.** Two rules drawn from the evidence remove unfinished games without adding a modern rule. Master is the game of skill. Contest is calmer, close to Kendall in hits and length.

### What the depictions add

Every depiction of play we could trace is catalogued in `depictions.md`: 30 entries from Dynasty 3 to Dynasty 26, checked against Porter & Moss. Twenty-two scenes were read image by image; seven reported scenes do not exist where the secondary lists put them, one is a mislabel, and one (Kairer) has no free image. The findings, with confidence:

- **Seven a side in the Old Kingdom.** Hesy-Re paints 7 and 7 pieces in a tray (high); Kaemankh shows all 14 on the board in one row (high); Mereruka shows 6 and 6 on the board and one more in each player's hand (medium).
- **Alternating order in most Old Kingdom scenes.** Kaemankh's 14 alternate from piece 3 to 12 (high); Mereruka's 12 alternate without a break (high); Rashepses' 11 alternate across the middle eight (medium); Meir's first five alternate (high), then break where the player's hand hovers, as in a game under way. Two Old Kingdom rows show one kind only (Nebkauhor, Idu pair B). The Middle Kingdom boards at Beni Hasan are mixed: in tomb 17 one board is grouped 5 and 5 by side (medium), which also shows 5 a side by Dynasty 11.
- **Mixed order in the New Kingdom and after.** Sennedjem's door (Dynasty 19) alternates strictly, 5 white spools and 5 red cones (high). Nefertari's row is 5 cones then 5 spools, Nakht's 5 spools then 5 cones, and Ani's is grouped by shape; all four are solo funerary scenes. The British Museum lion and gazelle (Ramesside) has 4 pieces of each kind grouped on the owner's side and a fifth in each player's hand. The Walters lintel (Dynasty 26) shows 10 like pieces, not alternating. The art does not fix a New Kingdom start.
- **Knucklebones in New Kingdom scenes.** The Imenmes game box (Louvre E 2710) shows two knucklebones under the board's stand (high on shape; the Louvre catalogue lists an osselet), and on the British Museum papyrus the lion holds an object the museum calls a knucklebone die (low from the image). Hesy-Re's Dynasty 3 grave goods show four sticks instead.
- **One player in funerary scenes, two in daily life.** Nefertari, Ani and Hunefer play alone; every Old and Middle Kingdom scene read, and Medinet Habu, has two.
- **Marked squares in Dynasty 3.** Hesy-Re's plan view marks squares 1, 11, 21, 27 and 30 (position high). A colour copy of the same facsimile shows a star on 1, two strokes on 11, five dashes on 21, four loops on 27 and one stroke on 30, the "one" of later Old Kingdom boards (sign identities low to medium).

## 6. The app

The app is at https://thegerm.github.io/AI-Chart/Projects/GERM-DEV-0031/. It offers ten rule sets: Minimal, Kendall 1978, Jéquier, Bell 1960, Piccione and the Germain Rules in five versions (Fate, Contest, Master, Old Kingdom Contest, Old Kingdom Master). Play is against the computer at easy, normal or hard, or two players on one screen.

**Evidence tags.** Every rule in every rule set carries a tag and a source note.

- **Evidenced:** shown by an ancient source (a board, a find, a tomb scene or a text).
- **Inferred:** read from ancient material that does not state it, for example square 27 as a penalty from the drowning text.
- **Invented:** no ancient basis; supplied by a modern author or by the app.
- **Unverified:** a citation or claim not checked at source for this project.

**Board and pieces.** The board glyphs are drawn after Carter 345: ankh-was on 15, three nfr signs on 26, water on 27, three birds on 28, two men on 29. Square 30 carries a sun disc in the app, as on Dyn 19 boards; on Carter 345 it is plain. The pieces are cones and spools.

**Engine.** Every disputed rule is a parameter in the engine. The code has 12 Node rule tests, the simulation script and a CI workflow.

**App-specific choices**, each tagged in the rule sets:

- Cones throw first.
- Kendall's "lose a turn" on 27 is omitted.
- Jéquier's points for pieces left on the board are omitted.
- Bell's surviving pieces count as borne off.
